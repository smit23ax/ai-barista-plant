AI Barista Plant: derivations

Every unit in the professor's format: a numbered Write line (the equation) and a Say line (plain words), the sign convention, a degree-of-freedom analysis, physical checks and likely questions. The same steps drive the app's Presenter view. Numbers are for the standard recipe: 800 µm grind, 93 °C kettle, 4 min steep, 15 g coffee in 250 g water.

1. Plant overview: what the plant does and its degrees of freedom

Sign convention. Mass or heat into the system is positive and out of it is negative, so a loss term carries a minus sign.

Degrees of freedom

  • Unknowns: strength TDS; extraction yield EY; dissolved mass m_l; beverage mass m_bev
  • Equations: TDS definition; EY definition; extraction model for m_l; beverage mass balance
  • Specified: the 4 setpoints the operator controls: grind d, kettle temperature T_w, steep time t_s, brew ratio R_w (the coffee dose m_c is fixed)
  • Result: 4 unknowns − 4 equations = 0 once the 4 setpoints are given. So the operator (human or AI) has exactly 4 degrees of freedom to choose: that's the AI's search space.

Steps

1. The plant in one line

Write:

$$\text{kettle} \rightarrow \text{press} \rightarrow \text{filter} \rightarrow \text{cup}$$

Say: Hot water from the kettle goes into the press, the grounds steep, the plunger filters them out, and fine particles settle in the cup. Each step is one unit with one main equation from our courses: a material balance, an energy balance, Darcy's law and Stokes' law.

2. Strength (what a TDS meter reads)

Write:

$$\mathrm{TDS} = \frac{m_l}{m_{bev}}$$

Say: TDS is total dissolved solids: the mass of coffee dissolved, m_l, over the mass of the drink, m_bev. The target is 1.15 to 1.35 percent.

3. Extraction yield

Write:

$$\mathrm{EY} = \frac{m_l}{m_c}$$

Say: Extraction yield is the same dissolved mass m_l, but over the dry coffee mass m_c. It says how much of the coffee we pulled out. The target is 18 to 22 percent.

4. Linking them (how you measure EY)

Write:

$$\mathrm{EY} = \mathrm{TDS} \cdot \frac{m_{bev}}{m_c}$$

Say: Divide the first definition by the second. A TDS meter plus a kitchen scale gives you EY. That's how the AI's measurements are built.

5. Degree-of-freedom count

Write:

$$N_{DOF} = N_{var} - N_{eq}$$

Say: Degrees of freedom are the number of variables minus the number of independent equations. Whatever is left over has to be chosen by someone.

6. The operator's choices

Write:

$$N_{DOF} = 4: \; (d,\; T_w,\; t_s,\; R_w)$$

Say: With the coffee dose fixed, the model leaves four free choices: grind size d, kettle temperature T_w, steep time t_s and brew ratio R_w. Those four dials are what the human and the AI turn. Everything else follows from the balances.

Physical checks

  • Mass balance: dissolved plus undissolved solubles always equals the extractable total (closure error about 1e-15).
  • If steep time goes to zero, m_l goes to zero, so TDS and EY both go to zero.
  • More water for the same coffee (higher R_w) lowers TDS but barely changes EY: dilution, not extraction.

Likely questions

Q1. What is the difference between TDS and EY?
Same numerator (dissolved mass). TDS divides by the drink, EY by the dry coffee.

Q2. Why 4 degrees of freedom and not more?
Properties come from temperature, and constants come from the literature. With the dose fixed, only grind, temperature, steep time and ratio are left.

Q3. Why can't you just set TDS directly?
TDS is an output. You can only set the inputs, and two inputs (grind, time) interact, which is why an optimizer helps.

2. R-101 extraction: three-pool material balance

Sign convention. Mass or heat into the system is positive and out of it is negative, so a loss term carries a minus sign.

Degrees of freedom

  • Unknowns: F(t); S(t); m_l(t); T(t) (from the energy balance)
  • Equations: balance on the surface pool; balance on the in-grain pool; balance on the dissolved pool; energy balance
  • Specified: grind d, kettle temperature T_w, steep time t_s, ratio R_w, coffee dose m_c; literature constants x_E, c_sat, tau_s,ref and tau_d,ref
  • Result: 4 unknown functions − 4 ODEs = 0, plus 4 initial conditions. Then TDS and EY follow from m_l.

Steps

1. Where the solubles sit at the start

Write:

$$M_E = x_E\, m_c, \quad F(0) = \phi M_E, \quad S(0) = (1-\phi) M_E$$

Say: Only x_E, about 30 percent of roasted coffee, can dissolve. Grinding breaks cells at the grain surface. That fraction phi is exposed and dissolves fast (pool F). The rest is locked inside the grain and must diffuse out (pool S).

2. Fraction in broken surface cells

Write:

$$\phi = 1 - \left( 1 - \frac{2 d_c}{d} \right)^3$$

Say: A shell one cell thick (d_c = 30 micrometres) around a sphere of diameter d. One minus the inner core's volume fraction is the shell. At 800 micrometres phi is about 21 percent: finer grind, more broken cells.

3. Surface pool: dissolution

Write:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{F}{\tau_s}\left( 1 - \frac{c}{c_{sat}} \right)$$

Say: Accumulation equals minus what dissolves. First order in F with time scale tau_s, and it slows as the liquid approaches saturation c_sat. With c at about 11 kilograms per cubic metre against 212, that factor stays near 1.

4. In-grain pool: diffusion out

Write:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{V_k}{\tau_d}\,(c_k - c)$$

Say: Solubles leave the grains at a rate driven by the concentration difference between the liquid inside the pores, c_k = S/V_k, and the bulk, c. tau_d is the diffusion time scale. If c_k equals c, the transfer stops.

5. Dissolved pool: what the other two lose

Write:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}m_l}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} - \frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t}, \quad c = \frac{m_l}{V_b}$$

Say: Everything leaving the grains enters the liquid: no generation, no reaction. The bulk concentration is the dissolved mass over the bulk liquid volume V_b.

6. Conservation check

Write:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(F + S + m_l) = 0$$

Say: Add the three equations: the total is constant. The app checks this every batch numerically, and the error is about 10 to the minus 15.

7. Why the time scales depend on grind size

Write:

$$\tau_d \propto \frac{d^2}{D_{eff}}, \quad \tau_s \propto d^3$$

Say: The diffusion time grows with distance squared over diffusivity: halve the grind and diffusion is 4 times faster. For the surface time, d cubed is the scaling that fits both of Moroney's measured grinds. They're anchored at 42.2 s and 1.18 s for a 322 micrometre grind at 90 °C.

8. Why temperature matters (Stokes-Einstein)

Write:

$$D_{eff} = \frac{k_B T}{3 \pi \mu d_m}$$

Say: A dissolved molecule of size d_m moving through water is a tiny Stokes sphere: thermal energy k_B T pushes it and viscous drag 3 pi mu d_m resists. Hot water has a lower mu and a higher T, so diffusion speeds up. Both time scales are multiplied by mu over T.

9. Solve and report

Write:

$$\mathrm{TDS} = \frac{m_l(t_s)}{m_{bev}}, \quad \mathrm{EY} = \frac{m_l(t_s)}{m_c}$$

Say: The equations are coupled and temperature changes with time, so scipy's solve_ivp integrates them to the steep time. For the standard recipe that gives TDS 1.10 percent and EY 17.9 percent, just under the ideal box.

10. The ceiling on extraction

Write:

$$\mathrm{EY}_{max} = x_E \frac{V_b}{V_b + V_k}$$

Say: At equilibrium c_k = c everywhere, so the liquid trapped in the grounds holds the same concentration and is thrown away. That caps EY at about 28.5 percent, not 30.

Physical checks

  • At t = 0 nothing is dissolved, so TDS = 0. As t gets large, EY approaches EY_max.
  • Doubling d makes tau_d 4 times longer, so coarse grinds need much longer steeps.
  • If c reached c_sat, surface dissolution would stop. In coffee c is far below, so it never does.
  • Units: F/tau_s is kg/s, and (V_k/tau_d)(c_k − c) is (m^3/s)(kg/m^3) = kg/s.

Likely questions

Q1. Why two pools and not one?
Moroney's data show a fast early rise then a slow tail: two mechanisms with time scales about 40 times apart.

Q2. Why is the model 'lumped'?
Real diffusion is a PDE inside each grain. I replace it with one time constant per pool, which is the undergraduate-level simplification, and I state it.

Q3. Where do your constants come from?
Moroney et al. 2016 (time scales, x_E, c_sat, cell size) and Corrochano et al. 2015 (densities, porosity).

Q4. What does 'well mixed' assume and is it valid?
One concentration throughout the liquid. It's reasonable after a stir, and it's the standard assumption in Moroney's model. A real press has some gradients, which is a stated limitation.

3. R-101 energy balance: mixing and cooling

Sign convention. Mass or heat into the system is positive and out of it is negative, so a loss term carries a minus sign.

Degrees of freedom

  • Unknowns: mixed temperature T_0; brew temperature T(t)
  • Equations: adiabatic mixing balance; transient lumped energy balance
  • Specified: masses and specific heats of water, grounds and vessel; T_w; room temperature; UA
  • Result: 2 unknowns − 2 equations = 0.

Steps

1. Mixing: heat lost by water = heat gained by grounds and glass

Write:

$$m_w c_{p,w} (T_w - T_0) = m_c c_{p,c} (T_0 - T_\infty) + m_v c_{p,v} (T_0 - T_v)$$

Say: Right after pouring, in the first seconds, heat loss to the room is negligible, so mixing is adiabatic. The hot water cools to T_0, while the room-temperature grounds and the press vessel warm up to T_0.

2. Total heat capacity

Write:

$$C = m_w c_{p,w} + m_c c_{p,c} + m_v c_{p,v}$$

Say: Add up mass times specific heat for everything that changes temperature together: water, grounds and glass. C is about 1370 joules per kelvin, and the glass is a fifth of it.

3. Solve for the starting temperature

Write:

$$T_0 = \frac{m_w c_{p,w} T_w + m_c c_{p,c} T_\infty + m_v c_{p,v} T_v}{C}$$

Say: A heat-capacity-weighted average temperature. With a cold 350 gram glass press, 93 °C water starts the brew at only 76.5 °C. Preheating the press (T_v = T_w) removes most of that drop.

4. Transient energy balance on the press

Write:

$$C \frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = -UA\,(T - T_\infty)$$

Say: Accumulation equals minus the heat lost to the room. Lumped: one temperature for everything, because the brew is stirred and the glass is thin. UA is the overall conductance, assumed to be 1 watt per kelvin.

5. Separate and integrate

Write:

$$T = T_\infty + (T_0 - T_\infty)\, e^{-t/\tau_{th}}, \quad \tau_{th} = \frac{C}{UA}$$

Say: Exponential decay toward room temperature with time constant tau_th. C is about 1370 joules per kelvin, so tau_th is about 23 minutes and a 4 minute steep loses about 9 degrees.

Physical checks

  • At t = 0, T = T_0. As t gets large, T approaches room temperature.
  • Double UA (a thinner glass or a draft) and the brew cools twice as fast.
  • Heating the brew back up would need a positive term: a heater, which the press doesn't have.

Likely questions

Q1. Why is mixing adiabatic but brewing is not?
Mixing takes seconds and cooling takes tens of minutes, so over the first seconds the loss is negligible.

Q2. When is the lumped model valid?
When the temperature inside is nearly uniform: stirred liquid and a thin wall. A Biot-number argument would make it formal.

Q3. Which numbers are assumed?
UA, the specific heat of the grounds and the vessel mass. Water properties come from CoolProp.

4. F-101 plunge filter: Darcy's law and Kozeny-Carman

Sign convention. Forward (the flow direction) is positive. Pressure drop is counted positive in the flow direction.

Degrees of freedom

  • Unknowns: superficial velocity u; permeability k; Sauter diameter d32; cake thickness L_c; pressure drop dP; plunger force F_p
  • Equations: u = V_l / (A_c t_p); Kozeny-Carman; Sauter mean; cake mass / bulk density; Darcy plus mesh in series; F_p = dP A_c
  • Specified: grind d, fines fraction w, plunge time t_p, coffee dose, porosity, mesh resistance, temperature
  • Result: 6 unknowns − 6 equations = 0.

Steps

1. Superficial velocity

Write:

$$u = \frac{V_l}{A_c\, t_p}$$

Say: The liquid volume pushed through in the plunge time, over the full press area. It's 'superficial' because it pretends the grounds aren't there. About 2 millimetres per second.

2. Darcy's law for the cake

Write:

$$\Delta P = \frac{\mu\, u\, L_c}{k}$$

Say: For slow flow through a porous bed, the pressure drop is proportional to viscosity, velocity and cake thickness, and inversely proportional to permeability k. It's the porous-media version of Hagen-Poiseuille.

3. Pores as tiny pipes: hydraulic radius

Write:

$$R_h = \frac{\varepsilon}{a_v (1-\varepsilon)}, \quad a_v = \frac{6}{d_{32}}$$

Say: The hydraulic radius is pore volume over wetted surface. epsilon is the void fraction, and a_v is the surface area per particle volume, 6 over d for a sphere.

4. Hagen-Poiseuille in a winding pore

Write:

$$u_i = \frac{R_h^2\, \Delta P}{2 \mu L_e}, \quad u_i = \frac{u\, \tau_t}{\varepsilon}, \quad L_e = \tau_t L_c$$

Say: In a round tube, the mean speed is D squared Delta P over 32 mu L, and D = 4 R_h gives R_h squared over 2. The real path L_e winds, with tortuosity tau_t, and the liquid moves faster in the pores than u by tau_t over epsilon.

5. Kozeny-Carman permeability

Write:

$$k = \frac{d_{32}^2\, \varepsilon^3}{36 K_K (1-\varepsilon)^2} = \frac{d_{32}^2\, \varepsilon^3}{180 (1-\varepsilon)^2}$$

Say: Combine the last two steps and compare with Darcy. The shape factor and tortuosity squared make the Kozeny constant K_K of about 5, so 36 times 5 gives 180. Permeability goes as d squared: finer grounds are much harder to push through.

6. Fines and the Sauter mean diameter

Write:

$$\frac{1}{d_{32}} = \frac{w}{d_f} + \frac{1-w}{d}$$

Say: Permeability is set by surface area, and fines carry a huge share of it. Just 15 percent fines by mass at 30 micrometres pulls d32 from 800 down to about 165 micrometres.

7. Cake plus mesh in series, then force

Write:

$$\Delta P = \mu u \left( \frac{L_c}{k} + R_m \right), \quad F_p = \Delta P\, A_c$$

Say: Like resistors in series, the cake and the mesh add. Pressure times plunger area is the force. It's about 2 newtons for the standard recipe. The mesh resistance and porosity are assumed values, so this is an estimate.

Physical checks

  • Halving the plunge time doubles u and so doubles the force: Darcy is linear in velocity.
  • As epsilon goes to 1 (no grounds), k becomes infinite and the cake adds no resistance.
  • Pore Reynolds number is about 0.9, inside Darcy's creeping-flow range.
  • Units: (Pa*s)(m/s)(m)/(m^2) = Pa.

Likely questions

Q1. Where does 180 come from?
36 from the hydraulic-radius algebra (6 squared) times the Kozeny constant of about 5.

Q2. Is Kozeny-Carman accurate for coffee?
No. Corrochano 2015 found it over-predicts coffee-bed permeability by about 1.3 to 6 times, so I treat it as an estimate.

Q3. Why does a too-fine grind make the press hard to push?
k goes as d32 squared, and fines drag d32 down, so the pressure drop shoots up.

5. T-101 cup: fines settling (Stokes' law)

Sign convention. Downward is positive. Every force is a signed number in that direction.

Degrees of freedom

  • Unknowns: terminal velocity v_t
  • Equations: force balance on one particle at terminal velocity (sum of forces = 0)
  • Specified: particle diameter d_p, particle density rho_p, brew density rho, brew viscosity mu (from temperature), gravity g
  • Result: 1 unknown − 1 equation = 0 degrees of freedom, so v_t is fixed once the specified variables are given.

Steps

1. Picture and sign convention

Write:

$$\text{downward} = +$$

Say: Picture one tiny fine of ground coffee, about 30 micrometres across, sinking in the cup. I pick downward as positive, and every force is a signed number in that direction. Three forces act: gravity pulls it down, buoyancy pushes it up, and drag from the liquid pushes up against its motion.

2. Newton's second law on the particle

Write:

$$m_p \frac{\mathrm{d}u_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = \Sigma F = F_g + F_b + F_d$$

Say: Mass times acceleration equals the sum of the forces. m_p is the particle's mass and du_p/dt is how fast its speed changes. Released from rest, it speeds up until the forces cancel.

3. Terminal velocity: the force balance

Write:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}u_p}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_g + F_b + F_d = 0$$

Say: At terminal velocity the speed stops changing, so acceleration is zero and the forces balance. That's a force balance, the same idea as the pipe flow balance, just on a sphere instead of a cylinder of fluid.

4. Particle volume

Write:

$$V_p = \frac{\pi d_p^3}{6}$$

Say: The volume of a sphere written with the diameter d_p instead of the radius: four thirds pi r cubed, with r equal to d_p over 2.

5. Weight (gravity)

Write:

$$F_g = +\rho_p V_p g = +\rho_p g \frac{\pi d_p^3}{6}$$

Say: Weight is mass times g, and mass is density times volume. rho_p is the wet particle density. It's positive because gravity points down, our positive direction.

6. Buoyancy (Archimedes)

Write:

$$F_b = -\rho V_p g = -\rho g \frac{\pi d_p^3}{6}$$

Say: Buoyancy equals the weight of liquid the particle pushes aside. rho is the density of the brew. The minus sign means it points up.

7. Drag in creeping flow (Stokes drag)

Write:

$$F_d = -3 \pi \mu d_p v_t$$

Say: For very slow flow around a sphere, drag is 3 pi times viscosity times diameter times speed. mu is the viscosity, the liquid's resistance to layers sliding past each other. Drag opposes the motion, so with the particle moving down it points up and is negative. The 3 pi comes from solving the creeping-flow equations around a sphere: two thirds of it is from viscous shear on the surface and one third from pressure.

8. Substitute into the balance

Write:

$$\rho_p g \frac{\pi d_p^3}{6} - \rho g \frac{\pi d_p^3}{6} - 3 \pi \mu d_p v_t = 0$$

Say: Put the three forces into the force balance: weight minus buoyancy minus drag equals zero.

9. Group the first two terms

Write:

$$(\rho_p - \rho)\, g \frac{\pi d_p^3}{6} = 3 \pi \mu d_p v_t$$

Say: Weight and buoyancy share the same volume, so they combine into the density difference times g times volume. That's the net downward pull, and it equals the drag.

10. Solve for the terminal velocity

Write:

$$v_t = \frac{(\rho_p - \rho)\, g\, d_p^2}{18 \mu}$$

Say: Divide both sides by 3 pi mu d_p. Pi cancels, one d_p cancels, and 6 times 3 gives 18. This is Stokes' law.

11. Wet particle density

Write:

$$\rho_p = \rho_s (1 - \varepsilon_p) + \rho\, \varepsilon_p$$

Say: A coffee fine is porous and its pores fill with water. Its density is the solid's density, rho_s = 1337 kilograms per cubic metre, times the solid fraction, plus water's density times the pore fraction epsilon_p = 0.53. That gives about 1145 kilograms per cubic metre, only a bit heavier than the brew, which is why fines settle slowly.

12. Check the assumption: particle Reynolds number

Write:

$$\mathrm{Re}_p = \frac{\rho v_t d_p}{\mu} < 0.1$$

Say: Stokes drag assumes creeping flow, where viscous forces dominate inertia. The particle Reynolds number compares the two. For a 30 micrometre fine at the end of the brew it's about 0.014, below 0.1, so Stokes' law holds. The app flags any case above 0.1.

13. Settling time through the cup

Write:

$$t_{set} = \frac{h_c}{v_t}$$

Say: The time to fall the depth h_c of the cup at constant speed. For 30 micrometre fines in 8 centimetres of coffee at the end of the brew (about 68 °C), v_t is about 0.2 millimetres per second, so settling takes about 7 minutes. That's why the last sip of French press coffee is gritty.

Physical checks

  • If rho_p = rho, v_t = 0: a particle with the liquid's density is neutrally buoyant and doesn't sink.
  • If rho_p < rho, v_t is negative: the particle rises (negative means up in our convention), like coffee oils and foam.
  • Doubling d_p makes v_t four times faster (d squared), so big grounds sink at once and fines linger.
  • Hotter brew has lower mu, so v_t rises: water's viscosity at 80 °C is about 40 % of its value at 25 °C.
  • Units: (kg/m^3)(m/s^2)(m^2) / (Pa*s) = m/s.

Likely questions

Q1. Where does the 18 come from?
6 from the sphere's volume, pi d^3/6, times 3 from the Stokes drag, 3 pi mu d v.

Q2. Why does Stokes' law fail for large particles?
Larger, faster particles have Re_p well above 0.1, so inertia matters and drag is no longer linear in speed. You'd need a drag-coefficient correlation instead.

Q3. Why does hot coffee settle faster?
Viscosity drops strongly with temperature, and v_t is proportional to 1/mu.

Q4. What happens if a particle is lighter than the liquid?
The density difference is negative, so v_t is negative and the particle rises.

Q5. Why use the wet density and not the solid density?
Water fills the particle's pores and moves with it, so the effective mass includes that water.

Q6. Does the cup wall or other particles matter?
I assumed one sphere far from walls and other particles. Fines are dilute and much smaller than the cup, so the effect is small. It's a stated assumption.

6. AI operator: Gaussian process and Bayesian optimization

Sign convention. Higher probability of hitting the target box is better. The AI maximises P_in.

Degrees of freedom

  • Unknowns: the next recipe (4 setpoints)
  • Equations: choose the recipe that maximises P_in over 3000 random candidates
  • Specified: all batches brewed so far (recipe, measured TDS, measured EY), the target box
  • Result: The 4 operator degrees of freedom from the plant overview are fixed by an optimisation, not by an equation.

Steps

1. What the AI sees

Write:

$$y = g_{true}(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon_n$$

Say: The AI doesn't know the physics. It sends a recipe x and gets back a noisy measurement y of TDS or EY, like a real operator reading a meter. The noise epsilon_n is the meter's scatter.

2. Similarity between recipes: the kernel

Write:

$$\delta = \sqrt{\sum_j \left( \frac{x_j - x'_j}{\ell_j} \right)^2}$$

Say: Two recipes are close if their setpoints are close, measured in units of a length scale ell_j for each setpoint. A long length scale means that setpoint barely matters. The AI learns them from data.

3. Matérn 5/2 kernel

Write:

$$\kappa(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sigma_f^2 \left( 1 + \sqrt{5}\,\delta + \frac{5}{3}\delta^2 \right) e^{-\sqrt{5}\,\delta}$$

Say: Similarity is 1 for identical recipes and fades with distance. It encodes 'similar recipes give similar cups', with smoothness like a real physical response. Plus a noise term sigma_n for the meter.

4. Prediction with uncertainty

Write:

$$y(\mathbf{x}) \approx \hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) \pm \hat{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})$$

Say: From the batches so far, each Gaussian process gives a best guess y-hat and how unsure it is, sigma-hat. Near brewed recipes sigma-hat is small, and far away it's large. scikit-learn does the matrix algebra.

5. Probability of landing in the box

Write:

$$P_{in} = \left[ \Phi\!\left( \frac{y_{hi} - \hat{y}}{\hat{\sigma}} \right) - \Phi\!\left( \frac{y_{lo} - \hat{y}}{\hat{\sigma}} \right) \right]_{\mathrm{TDS}} \times \left[ \text{same for EY} \right]$$

Say: For each outcome, the chance that a normal variable with mean y-hat and spread sigma-hat lands between the lower and upper target. Multiply the TDS and EY chances (treated as independent). The AI brews the recipe with the highest P_in out of 3000 random candidates.

6. Benchmark (best found, not a proof)

Write:

$$\text{AI } 20/20, \quad \text{one-factor-at-a-time } 6/20, \quad \text{random } 8/20$$

Say: 20 independent runs, at most 12 batches each. The AI reached the ideal box every time, with a median of 7 batches. One-at-a-time fails because grind and time interact.

Physical checks

  • With no data, every recipe is equally uncertain, so the AI explores. That's why it starts with 3 random batches.
  • Once one recipe hits the box, nearby recipes get high P_in, so the AI exploits.
  • The seed is fixed, so every benchmark run is reproducible.

Likely questions

Q1. Why a Gaussian process and not a neural network?
There are only a handful of data points, and I need uncertainty to decide what to try next. GPs give both.

Q2. What is the exploration vs exploitation trade-off?
Trying unknown recipes (high sigma-hat) versus refining good ones (good y-hat). P_in balances both automatically.

Q3. What are the weaknesses?
TDS and EY are treated as independent, it's only tested on the virtual plant, and GPs scale poorly beyond a few dimensions.
