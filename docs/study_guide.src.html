AI Barista Plant: study guide

Questions your professor is likely to ask, with short answers you should be able to give from memory. Each answer points to the section of derivations.html or the code that backs it. Practise saying them out loud.

A. The big picture

1. In one sentence, what is your project?

I modelled a French press as a small chemical plant (fluid flow, heat loss and mass transfer), then built an AI operator that uses that model's measurements to find the recipe that hits the ideal cup in as few batches as possible.

2. Where is the AI, and why is it needed?

The AI is the operator. It fits two Gaussian process models (one for strength, one for extraction yield) to the batches brewed so far, then picks the next recipe with the highest probability of landing in the ideal box. Each batch costs coffee and time, so the point is to learn from very few experiments. This is called Bayesian optimization [§10, ai.suggest].

3. If you have a physics model, why not just optimize it directly?

In the virtual plant I could. In a real kitchen the model's parameters (diffusion time, heat loss, mesh resistance) are uncertain, so the AI treats the plant as a black box and learns from measurements. That is exactly the situation in a real plant, where the model never matches reality perfectly.

4. Did the AI actually beat anything?

Yes. Over 20 independent runs with at most 12 batches, the AI hit the ideal box 20 out of 20 times (median 7 batches). One-factor-at-a-time managed 6 of 20 and random recipes 8 of 20. That is a best-found comparison for the strategies I tried, not a proof that the AI is optimal [scripts/benchmark.py].

5. Why does one-factor-at-a-time do badly?

Because the setpoints interact. Grind and steep time both control extraction, and the water ratio controls strength. Changing one at a time cannot see those interactions. A Gaussian process models all four together.

B. Material balance

6. Define TDS and extraction yield.

\(\mathrm{TDS} = m_l/m_{bev}\) is the strength: dissolved mass over beverage mass. \(\mathrm{EY} = m_l/m_c\) is dissolved mass over dry coffee mass. Combining them, \(\mathrm{EY} = \mathrm{TDS}\cdot m_{bev}/m_c\), which is how you get EY from a TDS meter and a scale [§1].

7. What are the three pools and why three?

Solubles on the broken-cell surface (dissolve fast), solubles inside the grains (must diffuse out slowly), and solubles dissolved in the coffee. Two release mechanisms with very different time scales need two pools. The third is where they end up [§3.1].

8. How do you know your mass balance is right?

The three rate equations add to zero, so \(F + S + m_l\) is constant by construction. The app checks it numerically every batch, and the closure error is about \(10^{-15}\) [§3.3, test_brew_mass_balance_closes].

9. Why can't extraction reach 30 %?

At equilibrium the concentration inside the grains equals the bulk concentration, so the liquid trapped in the spent grounds keeps some coffee: \(\mathrm{EY}_{\max} = x_E V_b/(V_b + V_k)\) [§3.5].

10. Why does a finer grind extract faster?

Diffusion time scales with distance squared: \(\tau_d \propto d^2/D_{eff}\). Halve the grain size and diffusion is four times faster. Finer grinds also have more broken cells at the surface [§3.4].

11. How does temperature come in?

Through the Stokes–Einstein relation \(D_{eff} \propto T/\mu\). Hot water is less viscous, so molecules diffuse faster. The viscosity comes from CoolProp, not typed-in numbers [§3.4].

C. Energy balance

12. Why does the brew start at about 76 °C when the kettle is at 93 °C?

Adiabatic mixing with cold grounds and a cold 350 g glass press: \(T_0 = \sum m c_p T / \sum m c_p\). Preheating the press removes most of that drop [§2.1].

13. Derive the cooling curve.

\(C\,dT/dt = -UA(T - T_\infty)\). Separate and integrate to get \(T = T_\infty + (T_0 - T_\infty)e^{-t/\tau_{th}}\), with \(\tau_{th} = C/UA \approx 22\) min [§2.2].

14. Which numbers did you assume rather than measure?

\(UA\), the grounds' specific heat, the mesh resistance, cake porosity, fines fraction, TDS meter noise, the discharge coefficient, and the pump curves. They're all listed in §11 and marked in the code. Be upfront about this: it shows you know the model's limits.

D. Fluid mechanics

15. Walk me through the pump calculation.

Mechanical energy balance: \(H_{sys} = \Delta z + h_f + h_m\). The friction loss comes from Darcy–Weisbach with \(f\) from Colebrook (solved with a root finder because it's implicit), and the fitting losses are \(\sum K\,V^2/2g\). The operating point is where the pump curve meets the system curve [§4].

16. Why did you not interpolate between laminar and turbulent friction factors?

Between Re 2300 and 4000 the flow is transitional and no correlation is reliable. The code raises an error rather than silently returning a number [physics.colebrook].

17. What is NPSH and why does it fail near boiling?

Net positive suction head available is the inlet pressure margin above vapour pressure, expressed as head: \(\mathrm{NPSH}_a = (P_{atm} - P_v)/\rho g + z_s - h_{L,s}\). Near 100 °C, \(P_v \to P_{atm}\), so the margin collapses and the pump cavitates. That's why pumps sit below hot tanks [§5].

18. What does the impeller Reynolds number tell you?

\(Re_i = \rho N D_i^2/\mu\) says whether stirring is laminar or turbulent. My extraction model assumes a well-mixed liquid, which needs turbulence, so the app warns below that [§6].

19. Explain Darcy's law and Kozeny–Carman.

Darcy: \(\Delta P = \mu u L/k\) for slow flow through a porous bed. Kozeny–Carman estimates \(k = d_{32}^2\varepsilon^3/(180(1-\varepsilon)^2)\) by treating pores as small Hagen–Poiseuille tubes sized by the hydraulic radius [§7].

20. Why the Sauter diameter, and why do fines matter so much?

Kozeny–Carman depends on surface area per volume, and \(d_{32}\) is the diameter with the same volume-to-surface ratio. Fines carry a huge share of the surface, so a small mass fraction of fines drags \(d_{32}\), and the permeability, way down [§7].

21. Is Kozeny–Carman accurate for coffee?

Not very. Corrochano et al. (2015) found it over-predicts measured coffee-bed permeability by a factor of about 1.3 to 6. I use it as an estimate and say so in the app. A real plunge on a kitchen scale would calibrate it [§7].

22. Derive Stokes' law and state when it is valid.

Weight minus buoyancy equals drag \(3\pi\mu d v\), which gives \(v_t = (\rho_p - \rho)g d^2/18\mu\). It's valid for particle Reynolds number below about 0.1, and the app flags anything above [§8].

23. Derive the carafe drain time.

Mass balance \(A_t\,dh/dt = -C_d a\sqrt{2gh}\) (Bernoulli for the jet speed). Separate and integrate: \(t_d = (A_t/C_d a)\sqrt{2h_0/g}\) [§9].

24. Where does fluid mechanics meet mass transfer in your project?

Grind size. Finer grounds extract faster (\(\tau_d \propto d^2\)) but are less permeable (\(k \propto d^2\)), so the press is harder to plunge and more fines end up in the cup. The best recipe is a trade-off between the two [§3.5, §7].

E. The AI in detail

25. What is a Gaussian process, without jargon?

A smooth curve-fitter that, besides predicting a value at a new recipe, says how unsure it is. It's confident near recipes already brewed and unsure far from them [§10].

26. What does the kernel do?

It encodes "similar recipes give similar cups". The Matérn 5/2 kernel says similarity decays with distance in setpoint space, with a separate length scale for each setpoint, learned from the data [§10].

27. How does the AI pick the next batch?

It computes, for 3000 candidate recipes, the probability that both strength and extraction fall inside the SCA box, using each GP's mean and uncertainty, and brews the most likely one [§10].

28. What is the exploration vs exploitation trade-off here?

Early on, every prediction is uncertain, so the highest-probability recipes are in unexplored regions (exploration). As data builds up, the AI concentrates near recipes that already look good (exploitation). The probability formula balances both automatically.

29. What are the weaknesses of your AI?

It assumes TDS and EY predictions are independent, it starts with three random batches, it has only been tested on the virtual plant, and Gaussian processes scale poorly to many dimensions. Four setpoints is fine.

30. How is this different from a PINN or ordinary neural network?

A neural network needs lots of data. Bayesian optimization is built for expensive experiments where you get only a handful of data points, and it gives calibrated uncertainty, which is exactly what deciding the next experiment needs.

F. Honesty questions (professors love these)

31. Is any of the data real?

Not yet. Every "measurement" is simulated from the model plus meter noise. The parameters come from published papers wherever possible (Moroney 2016, Corrochano 2015), and the rest are labelled assumptions. The next step is 5–10 real brews to validate.

32. A French press has no pump. Why is there a pump?

It's the plant "dress-up": a hypothetical transfer pump that lets the project cover pipe flow, Colebrook, system curves and NPSH, which a real plant handling hot water would need. The app labels it as hypothetical.

33. What would you do with more time?

Brew real batches to calibrate \(UA\), the mesh resistance and the diffusion time. Measure the grind distribution from a phone photo. Model a size distribution instead of one size. Add taste scores as a third AI objective.
